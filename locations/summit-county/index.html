Seven Thousand Feet Changes the Problem

Park City sits at roughly 7,000 feet. Annual snowfall routinely exceeds 300 inches in some years, temperatures can swing 50 degrees in a single day, and the range between a cold snap and a summer afternoon runs from about -15°F to 90°F. Materials that perform acceptably in Salt Lake City are being asked to do something different here.

High-altitude ultraviolet exposure compounds it. UV degrades roofing materials roughly 25% faster at Park City elevation than at valley locations, which shortens the practical service life of asphalt shingles and accelerates the breakdown of sealants at every penetration. The combination — deep snow load, violent thermal cycling, and accelerated UV ageing — is why mountain-grade specification is not an upsell in Summit County.

Ice Dams Last Half the Year

Ice-dam conditions at Park City elevation persist for five to six months, not for a cold week in January. That changes the engineering answer. Enhanced ice-and-water shield across the full roof deck, rather than the eaves-and-valleys minimum, is the recommendation we make here. IRC R905.1.2 sets the ice-barrier baseline; elevation is the reason to exceed it.

Ventilation matters for the same reason. An attic that stays warm feeds the melt-refreeze cycle at the eave. IRC R806.2 sets net free ventilation area at 1:150, or 1:300 with a vapor barrier.

A Narrow Installation Window

In most years, roof installation in Park City is limited to a June-to-October window. That single fact drives more Summit County scheduling decisions than anything else. A reroof identified in November usually waits for summer, which means the intervening winter has to be managed with repairs and monitoring rather than a replacement.

Project duration runs longer here too. Park City reroofs typically take three to seven days because of home complexity and elevation challenges, and HOA architectural approval can add two to four weeks on top of that.

Permits, Fire Rating, and Wind

Park City and Summit County both require building permits for roof replacements, and mountain building codes here carry enhanced requirements across three axes rather than one: snow load, wind resistance, and fire rating. The fire-rating requirement in particular catches homeowners who have only dealt with valley-floor projects before. Frame Restoration Utah handles code compliance as part of the project.

Storms Arrive From Above

Mountain thunderstorms bring intense hail to Park City, and they build fast over the peaks rather than tracking in across open valley. The practical consequence is that damage here is often concentrated and directional — one roof plane taking most of a storm — which is exactly the pattern that a ground-level glance misses and a documented inspection catches.

Specification at Altitude

Material selection in Park City answers three stresses at once. Snow load argues for metal panels or heavy architectural shingles on correctly engineered pitches. Thermal cycling argues for assemblies that tolerate repeated expansion and contraction without opening joints. Accelerated ultraviolet exposure argues against thin-gauge, low-spec products of any kind, because the sun here consumes their service life measurably faster.

Fire rating belongs in the same conversation. Mountain building codes in Summit County carry fire-rating requirements alongside snow and wind, and that constrains the material list in ways that valley-floor projects do not experience. Class 4 impact-resistant products address the hail side, and the underlayment specification — full-deck ice barrier rather than eaves-only — addresses the five-to-six-month ice-dam season.

Documenting Directional Hail Damage

Because Summit County storms build over the peaks and move fast, hail damage frequently lands on one or two roof planes rather than spreading evenly. A homeowner standing in the driveway sees an undisturbed slope and concludes nothing happened, while the opposite aspect has bruised mats, fractured granule beds, and dented soft metals at the vents, caps, and valley metal.

Our inspection photographs each roof plane separately and records the aspect, so the record reflects which slopes took the storm rather than averaging the roof into a single verdict. Coverage and claim decisions remain with the homeowner, carrier, and licensed insurance professionals; the photographs and the measured scope are what we contribute.

Resort-Town Logistics

Park City projects carry administrative weight that valley work does not. HOA architectural review is common and can add two to four weeks before a crew mobilizes, which means a reroof planned for the June-to-October window realistically needs its approvals started in spring. Access, staging, and material delivery on steep hillside lots and narrow older streets add further days to the schedule.

The practical advice for Summit County homeowners is unglamorous: get the inspection done early in the year, get the documentation and any HOA submission moving while the roof is still under snow, and hold the summer window for the work itself. Frame Restoration Utah also provides 24/7 storm response and emergency tarping for the months when a replacement simply cannot happen.
