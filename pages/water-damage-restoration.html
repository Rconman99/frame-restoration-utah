Why the clock matters

Water damage in a Utah home gets worse by the hour, and most of it happens where you cannot see it. Drywall wicks water upward like a paper towel. Hardwood starts to cup at the edges. Insulation holds moisture against the framing, and mold can begin on wet materials within a day or two. The first visit has one job: stop the water, get it out, and start drying before that second wave of damage sets in.

From first call to finished repair

A water damage restoration job in Utah follows the same sequence whether the water came from a split pipe in Ogden or an ice dam in Park City.

  1. 1. First hour on site

    Find the source and shut it off. Check for live electrical near the water. Photograph every wet room as found, then begin extraction so the standing water is gone before drying starts.

  2. 2. Day one

    Moisture mapping with meters and thermal imaging shows where the water traveled inside walls, under floors, and above ceilings. Materials that cannot be dried, like soaked carpet pad and drywall that wicked water, are cut out and recorded. Dehumidifiers and air movers are placed for the space.

  3. 3. Drying

    Readings are repeated at the same marked points on each return visit, and the equipment is moved as areas dry. Dense materials and cool basements take longer; the readings decide when drying is finished.

  4. 4. Repair

    Drywall, insulation, flooring, trim, and roof repairs follow once the structure is dry, written up as a scope separate from the emergency work.

Where the water usually comes from

Most water damage in Utah homes traces back to one of four sources:

  • Frozen and burst pipes. Cold snaps along the Wasatch Front and in the mountain towns split lines in exterior walls, crawlspaces, and garages, and the leak often starts when the pipe thaws.
  • Appliances and fixtures. Water heaters, washing machine hoses, dishwasher and refrigerator lines, and toilet supply lines fail without much warning.
  • Roof leaks and ice dams. Water through the ceiling after wind, hail, heavy snow, or an ice dam at the eave. We can repair the roof leak as well as the room below it.
  • Flooded basements. Snowmelt, storm runoff, and failed sump pumps. See flooded basement and flood cleanup.

Water damage questions we hear most

Do you really answer water damage calls 24/7?

Yes. Water and flood emergencies are answered 24/7 at 435-292-8802, including nights, weekends, and holidays. The same line covers emergency tarping and storm response. Routine roofing, fire and smoke, mold, and siding work is scheduled during business hours (Mon–Fri 8am–7pm, Sat 8am–6pm, Sun closed).

What should I do first when water is coming in?

Get everyone away from wet outlets and appliances, then shut off the main water valve if a pipe or appliance is the source. Call us next. If you can do it safely, take photos of each wet room before anything is moved.

How do you know when my home is actually dry?

We measure. Moisture readings are taken in the wet walls, floors, and framing on the first day and at the same marked points on every return visit, and the equipment stays until the materials reach a dry standard. In Utah's dry air a wall can feel dry to the touch while the stud behind it is still wet.

Can you repair the roof leak that caused the water damage?

Yes. We are a licensed roofing contractor, so when the water started at the roof, the leak and the interior damage it caused can go on one scope with one team.

Will my insurance cover water damage restoration?

That depends on your policy and on what caused the water. We give you photos, moisture readings, and a written scope to share with your insurer. Coverage decisions stay between you and your insurance company; we document and restore, we do not adjust claims.

Do you handle sewage backups?

No. Sewage backup cleanup is outside our scope. If a sewer or septic backup is involved, we will tell you so you can call a sewage cleanup specialist, and we can still help with clean-water damage and related repairs.
