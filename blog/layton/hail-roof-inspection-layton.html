What the September 18 warning actually said

The National Weather Service Salt Lake City bulletin issued at 2:24 p.m. MDT on September 18, 2026 included Layton and East Layton among the locations in a severe-thunderstorm warning. It covered parts of northeastern Davis County and adjacent counties, with a scheduled end time of 3:00 p.m. MDT. The bulletin identified a radar-indicated hail threat up to 1.25 inches.

That is a dated warning record, not a street-by-street hail measurement. It does not establish what fell on your house or whether roofing was damaged. This page is not a live alert service: use current NWS Salt Lake City alerts for ongoing conditions. The warning’s scheduled end time is not a guarantee that your location is safe.

During hail: shelter first, pictures later

Put personal safety ahead of checking the roof. Stay inside a substantial building, away from windows, while severe weather threatens. Follow current official instructions for your location. The NWS severe-weather safety guidance is the right starting point when thunder, hail, or damaging wind is nearby.

Do not walk outside to collect hailstones or place a ruler beside them during the storm. Do not use a ladder to get a closer picture. A wet roof, loose material, or a nearby power line can turn a simple check into an emergency. If someone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services rather than waiting for a roofing appointment.

Your first safe check: inside the house

Once you can safely assess occupied rooms, look for a new drip, damp patch, or change around a ceiling, skylight, or window. Describe the exact room and what you can observe. Avoid entering a questionable attic or standing under a sagging ceiling. Keep clear of water near electrical equipment; do not handle it or attempt an improvised repair.

If water is coming in, make that the first sentence of your call. Tell the team when you noticed it and whether it is continuing. Ask about availability and whether temporary protection should be considered. We do not promise an immediate arrival or that every leak can be resolved with a tarp. Access, conditions, and the actual roof need to be assessed.

No visible leak does not settle the condition of the exterior roof. Equally, an older ceiling stain is not proof that today’s hail caused it. Tell the inspector what you know and what you do not know; that distinction helps keep the assessment grounded in evidence.

Build a useful record without climbing

When conditions allow a safe ground-level look, make a short record of your property. Use ordinary descriptions rather than trying to diagnose an impact pattern yourself. Keep the originals and their dates, and avoid filters or edits that make marks look more pronounced.

  • Start wide: Photograph the side of the house or an accessible area so the location is understandable.
  • Then show the detail: Capture a fallen piece, an accessible metal mark, or an indoor stain from a stable position. Do not reach above a safe working height.
  • Separate time from cause: Record when you noticed the change. If you do not know whether a mark was already there, say so.
  • Label the building: Keep the house, garage, patio cover, and shed observations separate. One structure’s condition does not establish another’s.
  • Keep private details private: Send your address directly to the team when needed. Do not post access codes or personal information in public comments.

Dents on a vehicle, downspout, or other metal can be useful context. They do not, by themselves, establish the condition of shingles, underlayment, or a roof penetration. Avoid accepting a replacement recommendation based only on a photograph of hail in somebody else’s hand.

What to ask at a Layton hail roof inspection

Ask the roofer to distinguish observed conditions from possible causes. Useful findings identify the roof area, the material involved, and any limitation on what could be checked. Request photographs that correspond to the explanation rather than an unlabeled collection of close-ups.

Discuss the roof’s approximate age, previous repairs, and any pre-existing leak history if you know them. Do not guess a date or material specification to fill a blank. If the property includes several roof types or an addition, ask which sections were assessed and which were not.

Before authorizing work, ask for a written scope. Which conditions does it address? Why is a repair sufficient, or why is a larger project recommended? What is outside the proposed scope? A careful inspection can lead to different next steps; this article does not assume that replacement is necessary after every hailstorm.

For permit questions tied to actual proposed work, start with Layton City’s official website and confirm applicable requirements with the city and your contractor. A storm warning is not a permit determination. Keep any insurance coverage or documentation questions with your insurer; roofing findings do not guarantee a coverage decision.

Make your inspection request easy to act on

Tell us that the property is in Layton, whether water is actively entering, and how best to reach you. Include the date you observed the concern. If you already have safe photographs, say what they show. You do not need to wait until you have a perfect photo packet to report an active leak.

For example: “My home is in Layton. I noticed a new damp spot in the upstairs hallway after the September 18 storm. I have not checked the roof. Can we discuss an inspection?” Use only details that are true for your home. If there is no leak and your concern is an exterior mark, describe that instead.

If you rent the home or someone else manages it, coordinate with the person authorized to arrange access and work. A request submitted through this website is not a confirmed appointment. Confirm scheduling directly, and update the team if the condition changes while you are waiting.

Build a useful photo record without getting on the roof

A helpful photo sequence starts with context, not magnification. From a safe position, take an overview that shows the affected elevation, then a closer view of the concern. A porch column, garage doorway, chimney, or upstairs window gives the recipient a reference point. Avoid leaning over balconies, stepping onto slick patios, or standing beneath loose branches. If the only possible angle requires climbing, leave that picture for the inspection.

Group photographs by structure: house, detached garage, shed, or addition. Use plain labels such as “front entrance ceiling” or “garage wall beside driveway.” Compass directions are useful only when you know them. Explain whether a mark appeared recently or was already present. Preserve the unedited originals and timestamps; screenshots and compressed copies can remove useful detail. Make sure each attachment opens before sending it.

Protect privacy while documenting the concern. Keep children, license plates, account statements, security controls, and neighbors’ interiors out of shared photographs. If an image contains sensitive material, create a separate cropped copy and retain the original privately. Give the street address directly to the team during scheduling rather than publishing it in a comment. A public weather discussion is not an appropriate place for gate codes or an empty-home schedule.

Use an observation log instead of a damage diagnosis

A simple record can contain four columns: time, location, observation, and change since the previous check. For instance: “Upstairs hallway, fresh brown ceiling mark beside the light fixture, noticed after the afternoon storm, no dripping visible now.” This is a hypothetical example, not a customer report. It communicates more useful information than “roof destroyed” without asking the homeowner to identify the cause.

Separate what you heard, what you saw, and what somebody else told you. Loud impacts alone do not establish the condition of a shingle. A neighborhood photograph may establish that hail occurred where that photograph was taken, but does not show your roof. Similarly, an old stain and new dripping are different observations. Reporting uncertainty honestly helps the inspector focus the visit.

Do not touch wet switches, outlets, appliances, or electrical fixtures to investigate. If a ceiling looks unstable or a room appears unsafe, stay out. Describe the concern from a safe location and use emergency services for immediate danger. A roofing callback is not emergency electrical or structural assistance. If you cannot safely make an observation, “not checked because access looked unsafe” is an appropriate entry.

Prepare for a confirmed inspection visit

Collect existing paperwork that is easy to find: installation invoices, maintenance receipts, warranty documents, previous inspection summaries, or a dated photograph from before the storm. Missing paperwork should not delay reporting water entry. Mark approximate dates as approximate. Neither roof age nor the absence of a receipt establishes whether this particular storm caused damage.

Tell the scheduler about separate roof sections or materials on an addition, porch, or outbuilding. Mention solar equipment or other rooftop installations so access can be discussed appropriately. Do not disconnect panels, remove fasteners, lift shingles, or scrape sealants yourself. Ask whether the agreed inspection covers every structure you mentioned or only the immediate concern. This avoids assuming that a limited appointment is a complete property assessment.

Discuss practical access privately: a locked gate, loose pet, narrow driveway, occupied rental, or shared parking area. Confirm who should be present and how the team will communicate a scheduling change. If you are helping a relative or coordinating while traveling, establish one point of contact. An authorization to inspect is separate from permission to perform repairs; make sure the occupant and the person arranging the appointment understand the same scope.

Questions to ask while reviewing the findings

  • What did you observe? Request labeled photographs or a plain-language explanation, distinguishing a visible condition from a possible cause.
  • What could not be examined? Record inaccessible sections, visibility limitations, and any recommended follow-up visit.
  • Why this recommendation? Ask how the proposed repair addresses the finding and why a larger replacement would or would not be necessary.
  • What remains uncertain? Ask whether concealed conditions, material availability, or another assessment could change the proposed scope.
  • What should happen next? Confirm the person responsible for the next contact, any requested documents, and whether scheduling is tentative or confirmed.

Keep the estimate understandable and the records organized

Save the initial proposal and subsequent revisions as separate dated versions. If the scope changes, ask what observation or decision explains the revision. Compare inclusions and exclusions rather than totals alone. Removal, disposal, surrounding details, cleanup, and material identification may affect what a proposal actually covers. Ask for clarification if one document names a specific section while another simply says “repair roof.”

Appearance concerns should be discussed before agreeing to work. Ask what can be confirmed about matching an existing material and what remains dependent on supply. A screen image is not necessarily an accurate color sample. Do not assume a discontinued product is available or that every component comes with the same warranty. Request the relevant written terms and an explanation of who handles questions afterward.

Distinguish a proposed start date, an estimated duration, and a reserved appointment. Weather, access, materials, and newly discovered conditions can affect a schedule, so agree on the communication process. If temporary protection is discussed, clarify its intended purpose and limitations rather than treating it as permanent repair. Keep the contractor’s contact information accessible if conditions change.

A tidy folder can contain observations, originals, appointment confirmations, findings, proposals, and correspondence. Label documents consistently enough that another household member could find the latest version. Keep policy numbers, private access instructions, and payment information secure. This organization does not determine insurance coverage; it simply makes accurate information easier to retrieve when your contractor or insurer asks a question.

A final check before you send the first request

Include your actual town, preferred callback method, whether water is actively entering, and the structure involved. Attach only safe, relevant pictures and ask for receipt confirmation. Explain when you are available to discuss access. If the request concerns a managed property, identify the appropriate scheduling contact without sharing unnecessary tenant information. You do not need a dramatic description or a complete diagnosis to ask for help—clear observations and an accurate location are the useful starting point.

Layton hail and roof inspection questions

Was Layton included in the September 18 hail warning?

Yes. The NWS Salt Lake City warning issued at 2:24 p.m. MDT on September 18, 2026 listed Layton and East Layton. Its 1.25-inch hail threat was radar-indicated, not a measurement from every property.

Does 1.25-inch hail mean I need a new roof?

No. A warning describes a weather threat, not a roof condition. An inspection should identify actual findings and explain whether repair, further assessment, or replacement is appropriate.

Describe the active leak when you call. Avoid electrical hazards, unsafe attics, and areas under sagging ceilings. Ask about current availability and temporary protection; a website request does not reserve an immediate visit.
