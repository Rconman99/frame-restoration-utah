Common Storm Damage Types in Draper

Draper's elevation and location in the Wasatch Front mean spring and early summer storms can be severe. We commonly see three types of storm damage that deserve documented inspection:

  • Hail damage: Hail dents shingles, creating weak spots where water pools and eventually leaks. Even small hail can significantly shorten roof lifespan.
  • Wind damage: Strong winds tear shingles away, expose underlayment, and can damage flashing around chimneys and vents. Wind-driven damage is the most common claim we handle.
  • Tree damage: Falling branches or trees directly puncture roofs or cause widespread impact damage. Photograph the limb and roof condition before cleanup when it is safe to do so.

In Draper neighborhoods like Promontory and South Draper, wind and hail often hit upper-bench roof planes differently than valley-floor homes. Clear photos, soft-metal checks, and slope-by-slope notes help separate storm impact from age, wear, and maintenance issues.

When should I document storm damage?

Document immediately after the storm passes and you've confirmed it's safe to be outside. Capture photos or video while evidence is fresh, save the storm date, and get a professional inspection within the first few days when possible. If active water is entering the home, emergency mitigation comes first.

Step-by-step roof-condition documentation process

Understanding the documentation process reduces stress and helps you avoid costly mistakes. Here's how it works for roof damage in Draper:

1. Document Everything Immediately

Before the carrier representative arrives, take clear photos and video of all visible damage. Shoot from multiple angles in good lighting. Document damage to gutters, flashing, vents, and the ground around your home as well. Write down the date and time of the storm. These photos are your evidence if disputes arise later.

2. Review your carrier process

Policy notice windows and carrier steps vary. If you decide to request carrier review, provide the storm date, a brief description of visible damage, and the photos or contractor notes you already have. Keep the carrier's reference number and next-step instructions in the same roof-condition file.

What carrier representatives may review during inspections

Carrier representatives are trained to distinguish between storm impact, wear and tear, and maintenance conditions. Knowing what they may assess helps you prepare a better roof-condition file:

  • Sudden, visible impact: Adjusters look for evidence of a specific event. Hail marks, broken branches, or torn shingles indicate a storm caused damage.
  • Roof age and condition: Older roofs with pre-existing wear may be reviewed differently. Representatives examine granule loss, curled shingles, and moss growth.
  • Maintenance history: Gutter overflow, overhanging branches, prior leaks, and deferred repairs can complicate review because maintenance and storm impact may overlap.
  • Multiple damage points: Storm damage is usually widespread. Damage limited to one small area raises suspicion. Adjusters expect to see consistent damage patterns across the roof.
  • Interior damage: Water stains on ceilings or walls help establish when water entered and which roof area may be involved.

Common roof-documentation mistakes to avoid

Homeowners in Draper often make mistakes that make storm review and repair planning harder. Here's what to avoid:

How Different Storm Damage Gets Reviewed

Not all storm damage is reviewed the same way. Coverage depends on your policy language, cause of loss, deductible, exclusions, and carrier findings.

Sudden-event damage: Hail, high winds, falling trees, and lightning strikes are usually reviewed as storm events, but the carrier still determines whether the observed roof condition matches the policy.

Maintenance or age questions: Tree maintenance, old brittle shingles, prior leaks, and deferred repairs can complicate review because carriers may separate storm damage from pre-existing conditions.

Separate policy issues: Flood damage, interior water damage, code upgrades, and depreciation recovery can involve separate policy terms. Review your declarations page and ask your carrier before assuming an outcome.
