Safety and an active leak come before a roofing decision

Do not inspect a roof during a thunderstorm. Stay inside, away from windows, and follow official instructions. The NWS thunderstorm safety page explains shelter precautions. A photo is never worth walking under loose material or approaching a downed line.

From a safe indoor location, note any new water entry. Tell the roofing team which room is affected, whether water is still appearing, and when you first noticed it. Stay away from electrical hazards and visibly unstable ceilings; do not enter an unsafe attic. Immediate danger belongs with emergency services, not a web-form queue.

If you need temporary protection, ask what is feasible for the actual condition and access. Do not assume a tarp is suitable for every opening or that an appointment is reserved simply because you sent a message. Confirm the team’s availability and agreed next step. This guide does not promise round-the-clock response or a same-day visit.

Before requesting work, identify who can approve it

For a home you own and occupy, you may be the person coordinating the inspection. For a rental, attached home, or managed property, establish who is responsible before making arrangements. Ask which roof sections are private and which may be shared. Different ownership arrangements can require different contacts; this is a preparation question, not a claim about every Farmington neighborhood.

If a manager or association is involved, ask who can authorize access, receive the inspection findings, and approve any subsequent work. Those are separate decisions. A tenant’s report is important evidence of a concern, but it is not automatically authorization to replace materials. Keep communications clear so the roofer is not arriving with contradictory instructions.

Provide private access details only through an appropriate direct conversation. Do not place entry codes, tenant information, or an empty-home schedule in a public comment. Confirm a safe meeting point and any parking restrictions before a visit, without assuming a particular ladder or vehicle can reach the roof.

A short homeowner file helps prevent confused estimates

Collect what you already know instead of trying to become a roof inspector overnight. A useful file can be simple: the date of the storm concern, a few original photos taken safely, prior repair records if available, and a list of questions. Separate confirmed facts from recollections.

  • Roof history: Note an installation or repair date only if you have a record or a reliable recollection. “Unknown” is an acceptable answer.
  • Existing conditions: Mention earlier leaks or stains so the inspector can distinguish current observations from older concerns.
  • Accessible pictures: Pair a wide view with a detail, label the area, and retain the unedited original. Do not climb to complete the file.
  • Multiple structures: Label house and garage concerns separately; different roof sections can need different recommendations.
  • Correspondence: Save the appointment confirmation and any written findings in the same folder.

If you text photos, identify the property town and the subject of the pictures. Ask the team to confirm receipt. A sent attachment is not confirmation that its contents have been reviewed, and an online image alone may not establish the source or extent of a roofing problem.

Five questions to ask before approving hail-related repairs

1. What was actually observed? Ask the contractor to point out the location and explain the condition in everyday language. A statement that the neighborhood had hail is background, not a substitute for a finding about your roof.

2. What could not be checked? Access limitations, visibility, and current conditions can affect an inspection. Ask whether a recommendation depends on another visit or information that has not yet been obtained. Uncertainty should be stated, not hidden behind a confident conclusion.

3. Why this scope? If repair is proposed, ask which area it addresses. If replacement is proposed, ask why a limited repair would not address the identified need. Neither option is automatic because a warning mentioned Farmington.

4. What does the written estimate include? Look for understandable work descriptions, material identification, removal or disposal responsibilities, and exclusions. Compare the same scope when considering more than one proposal rather than treating the lowest or highest total as proof of quality.

5. What happens next? Confirm who will contact whom, how scheduling is agreed, and what questions remain unresolved. Discuss written terms before signing. If a salesperson pressures you to make a decision without findings or scope, take time to review the information.

Keep permits, coverage, and roof findings separate

For questions about city requirements that apply to a proposed repair or replacement, consult Farmington City’s building department. Confirm requirements for the actual project; do not assume a storm-related job is exempt or that a website article supplies approval.

Ask your insurer about your policy, its documentation requests, and any applicable reporting requirements. The roofing team can describe observed conditions and proposed work, but this article does not decide coverage, promise payment, or set a claim deadline. Keep inspection findings available without changing or exaggerating them to fit an expected outcome.

Contractor identity is another separate check. Use Utah DOPL to verify the contractor you are considering. Make sure the business you communicate with matches the written proposal. A storm-related offer should still be evaluated with the same care you would use for any home improvement.

A worksheet for comparing roofing proposals in Farmington

Set aside a page for each proposal and compare the same categories. A useful estimate should help you understand the decision rather than require you to decode a sales presentation. This worksheet is a discussion aid, not a contract template or a legal opinion. Ask for explanations when unfamiliar terminology appears, and keep unanswered questions visible until somebody has addressed them.

Define the structure and the boundary of the work

Check that the written proposal identifies the correct property and distinguishes the dwelling, detached garage, porch, addition, and other structures. Ask which roof planes or sections are included. “Repair roof” is difficult to compare with a description of a particular opening or damaged section. Clarify whether adjacent components are included, excluded, or mentioned only as background.

Separate observed conditions from recommended upgrades. An improvement may be worth considering, but it should not be presented as storm damage merely because it appears in the same document. Ask how a concealed condition would be documented if it becomes visible during construction, and how a change in scope would be approved. Avoid treating an allowance, an assumption, and a confirmed finding as interchangeable.

Understand the material specification

Where selections have been made, ask for the proposed manufacturer, product, and relevant description. Explain appearance preferences early, particularly when a limited repair meets an existing finish. Ask what is known about availability and what remains uncertain. A digital color sample may not reproduce the actual appearance, and this article cannot promise that a discontinued product or weathered surface can be matched.

When a proposal mentions flashing, underlayment, ventilation, edge details, penetrations, or transitions between materials, request an explanation of their role in the proposed work. You do not need to become a roofer to understand a recommendation. Do not remove shingles, sealants, fasteners, or rooftop equipment yourself to investigate. The purpose of this conversation is clarity, not a do-it-yourself inspection.

Discuss logistics before construction is scheduled

Ask where equipment, deliveries, and debris containers may go if work is approved. Mention shared parking, driveway limitations, landscaping, gates, pets, and normal household activity. If a manager or association controls a common area, coordinate with the responsible person before treating that area as available staging space. Confirm how occupants will be notified and whom to contact with a concern.

Discuss protection of nearby surfaces and the intended cleanup process. Keep children and animals away from work zones. Do not assume an inspection appointment includes construction, debris removal, or temporary protection. A written description helps avoid one person expecting a brief assessment while another expects a crew to begin repairs. Request clarification about disposal responsibilities and any exclusions that matter to your property.

Distinguish a schedule estimate from a reservation

A proposed start date, confirmed visit, and estimated duration describe different commitments. Ask which you have been given. Discuss how weather, material supply, access, or additional findings could affect scheduling without assuming a delay has already occurred. Confirm the preferred channel for updates and the appropriate contact if a leak changes before the appointment.

If temporary protection is proposed, ask about its purpose, limitations, and relationship to permanent work. Keep that agreement with the other records. Do not assume a temporary measure guarantees protection against every subsequent storm. If there is an immediate hazard, follow emergency guidance rather than waiting for an ordinary scheduling response.

Review identity, terms, and changes carefully

Compare the business named in the proposal with the company you contacted and the contractor identity you verified. Ask for an explanation of deposits, payment stages, exclusions, and change-approval procedures stated in the paperwork. This guide does not recommend payment terms or determine legal rights. Questions about a document’s legal effect belong with a qualified adviser.

Keep complete copies of documents you choose to sign. Preserve revisions as separate dated versions rather than overwriting the original estimate. If someone describes a form as routine, read it anyway and request clarification of anything you do not understand. A neighborhood weather event is not a reason to skip normal care when evaluating a home-improvement proposal.

Coordinate a shared roof without conflicting instructions

For a rental, townhome, or managed property, establish responsibility for the relevant roof section before arranging work. Do not infer responsibility from building appearance or a neighbor’s arrangement. The occupant reporting a stain may differ from the person who authorizes access, receives findings, or approves construction. Identifying those roles prevents contradictory instructions without assuming every Farmington property has the same ownership structure.

A coordination note can identify the reporting occupant, authorized scheduling contact, and recipient of the inspection summary. Keep private telephone numbers, entry codes, and tenant details out of public posts. Ask whether the manager wants photographs, a findings summary, or a proposed scope, and how the material should be delivered. Agree on a single point of contact when several relatives or representatives are involved.

Use consistent labels for units, buildings, and shared areas. Describe the indoor location of a concern without assuming the precise exterior entry point. Ask how inspected sections will be distinguished from inaccessible sections. If the appointment covers only part of the requested area, record that limitation and discuss the remaining work rather than treating a partial visit as a complete assessment.

Keep your information useful after the visit

Organize photographs, observations, appointment confirmations, inspection findings, estimates, and correspondence in a folder with clear filenames. Preserve original pictures even when you send smaller copies. A close-up may require a labeled overview to explain where it was taken. Retain dates and distinguish new observations from older records so the sequence is understandable to somebody who did not attend the visit.

Keep insurer communications separate from the contractor’s proposed price. An estimate is not a coverage decision, and an observed condition is not a reimbursement promise. Ask your insurer about its own documentation process without altering findings to fit an expected result. Protect policy identifiers, financial information, and access arrangements when sharing records.

Before deciding, answer these six questions

  • Can you explain the finding? You should understand the observed concern in ordinary language, not just repeat a technical label.
  • Can you locate the work? Ask for clarification when the proposal does not distinguish structures or sections.
  • Do the pictures have context? Request labels or an overview when an isolated close-up is hard to interpret.
  • Are limitations disclosed? Keep inaccessible areas and unanswered questions on the list rather than assuming they were resolved.
  • Are alternatives explained? Ask why the recommended scope fits the findings instead of expecting every roof to need an identical solution.
  • Is the next contact clear? Confirm who will call, what information remains necessary, and whether an appointment is actually reserved.

You do not need to decide every detail in the first conversation. Prioritize safety, accurate observations, and a documented next step. Farmington’s inclusion in the warning supplies event context; your repair decision still depends on the condition at your own address.
