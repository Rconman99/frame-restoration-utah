Why Snow Load Matters When Replacing a Roof in Heber City, and Who Decides the Number

Here is the direct answer to the question most Heber City homeowners never think to ask until a roofer mentions it. Snow load matters because a roof is not only a waterproof skin. It is also part of a structure that has to carry weight, and in a high mountain valley the weight that arrives every winter is snow. When you replace a roof, you are opening up the one part of the house that was designed around that weight. That is the moment to confirm that what goes back on is compatible with what the structure was designed to carry.

As for who decides the number: it is not the homeowner and it is not a salesperson with a clipboard. Utah State University publishes a statewide Utah Snow Load resource, and it explains that its predictions are calculated using an adaptation of PRISM, which it describes as a weighted linear regression model that uses elevation to calculate ground snow load at a local level. That gives a starting point for any address. The number that actually applies to a specific site is then decided by an engineer or by the building official who reviews the work.

If your project is a straight tear-off and re-cover on sound decking, the snow load conversation may be short. If the decking or framing will change in any way, ask about snow load out loud and expect a real answer from someone qualified to give it.

What Ground Snow Load Actually Means

Ground snow load is a design value. It describes the weight of snow that a site should be expected to see on the ground, and it is the starting point engineers use when they work out what a roof structure has to carry. It is a planning number rather than a forecast. It does not tell you how much snow will fall next week, and it does not change because one winter looked different from the last. It exists so that buildings are designed around a consistent, defensible figure instead of a feeling.

Homeowners often confuse snow load with snowfall. Snowfall is weather that changes every winter. Snow load is a design value that feeds into rafters, trusses, decking and connections. This post deliberately quotes no numbers, because the right figure for your property comes from the people authorized to determine it.

It also helps to know what snow load is not. It is not a shingle rating, and it is not a product warranty. A shingle can be a perfectly good product and still sit on a roof structure that was never reviewed for the load it carries. That is why a good roof replacement conversation separates the covering, which is what most people shop for, from the structure underneath, which is what the snow load number is really about.

How Utah State University's Map Predicts Snow Load From Elevation

The Utah Snow Load site run by Utah State University is the resource to know. Its own description says the predictions are calculated using an adaptation of PRISM, a weighted linear regression model that uses elevation to calculate ground snow load at a local level. In plain language, the map takes how high a location sits above sea level and uses that relationship to estimate the ground snow load there. Elevation is the driver.

That is what makes Utah different from places with flat, uniform snow country. Here, two addresses that are close together on a road map can sit at very different heights, and a model that is driven by elevation can reflect that. For a homeowner, the lesson is that you cannot borrow a number from a friend across the state, or even assume that a rule of thumb from another valley applies. The elevation of the site is what the model is built on, so the site is what matters.

Local building rules point to the same tool. Heber City is the county seat of Wasatch County, and Wasatch County's building design criteria, effective July 1, 2023, say the ground snow load is to be determined using the Utah State University calculator. If your home is inside Heber City limits, confirm with Heber City's building department which criteria apply to your address. Either way, the USU number is a reasoned starting point for an engineer or building official, not a substitute for the person who decides what applies to the house in front of them.

Where Engineering Judgment Enters: When the Site Doesn't Match the Map

The USU resource includes a sentence every homeowner should read. It states that engineering judgement must be exercised where the predicted elevation greatly differs from the actual elevation at the site. That is the map itself telling you that it is a prediction and that it has limits. When the elevation the model assumes is meaningfully different from the real elevation of the lot, a person with the training to judge it has to step in.

In a valley, that matters. A house on a rise, along a bench or at the edge of the valley floor may not sit at the elevation the model assumed for that spot. Where the two greatly differ, the source says engineering judgement is required.

This is the clearest reason the decision should not be made by a roofer alone. A roofer can observe the roof and the decking. A roofer cannot responsibly replace the judgment of an engineer or the authority of the building official. When Decking or Framing Changes, Ask About Snow Load

If you take only one habit from this post, make it this one: whenever decking or framing changes, ask about snow load. A tear-off often reveals what the old roof was hiding. Decking may be damaged, uneven or in need of replacement. Framing may need repair. The moment those parts of the structure are altered, you are no longer simply swapping a covering. You are touching the part of the roof that carries load, and the question of what snow load applies is fair to raise.

The questions are straightforward. Is the decking staying as it is or being replaced? Is any framing being repaired, added or modified? Has anyone confirmed with the building official whether a permit or review applies to this scope? Has an engineer been asked about the site if the situation calls for it? You do not need to know the answers in advance. You only need to be willing to ask, and to expect a clear reply from your contractor.

Questions to Ask Any Roofer Before You Sign in Heber City

Before you sign anything, ask whether the contractor holds a Utah contractor license and whether they carry insurance. Ask who is responsible for confirming what applies to the site when decking or framing changes. Ask whether the work follows a recognized standard such as IRC R905, and ask what the workmanship warranty covers and for how long. A contractor who is confident in their process will answer these without hesitation.

Then ask the snow load question itself, in your own words. Something like: given that Heber City sits high in the Heber Valley, who confirms the snow load that applies to my house, and what happens if my decking or framing needs to change? The best answer names an engineer or the building official as the decision-makers, not a guess. If a roofer waves the question away, that tells you something useful about how they work.

Finally, keep your own records. Photograph the roof before work begins, keep your written scope and any correspondence, and keep a copy of any documentation about the decking or framing.
